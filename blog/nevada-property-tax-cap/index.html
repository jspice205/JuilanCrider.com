Nevada’s 3% property tax cap: what Las Vegas buyers need to know

If you’re moving to Las Vegas or Henderson, you’ll hear that Nevada property taxes are “capped.” That’s true, but the cap is easy to misunderstand, and buyers who don’t claim it can end up paying more than they need to. Here’s what the cap actually does, based on how Clark County and its Assessor’s office explain it.

1. It caps the bill’s growth, not the value

Nevada’s tax abatement limits how much your property tax bill can go up from one year to the next. It is not a cap on your home’s value, and it doesn’t freeze your taxes. Your bill can still rise every year, just not faster than the cap allows.

2. Primary residence: 3%. Everything else: up to 8%

If you own and live in the home as your primary residence (a single-family home, townhouse, condo or manufactured home), the yearly increase on your tax bill is capped at 3% under NRS 361.4723. Homes that aren’t owner-occupied, like a rental or a second home, get a cap of up to 8%. The same up-to-8% cap applies to land and commercial property.

3. You can only have one primary residence in Nevada

The 3% cap is for the one home you actually live in. If you own more than one property in Nevada, only one of them can be your primary residence for the cap.

4. You have to claim it

The Assessor mails a postcard so owners can confirm the home is their primary residence. Sign it and send it back. If the home is listed at the wrong cap, the Assessor’s office can correct it, but there are deadlines, so don’t sit on it.

5. Changes in ownership matter

The Assessor ties the cap to who is on title. A sale, or adding or removing someone from the deed, can change it. As a buyer, plan on confirming your primary residence status with the Assessor after you close instead of assuming the seller’s cap carries over.

6. New construction works a little differently

A brand-new home, or a property whose use changes, doesn’t get a cap in its first fiscal year. The 3% or up-to-8% cap starts the year after.

What this means when you’re buying

Don’t budget off the seller’s current tax bill alone. Ask your lender to estimate the taxes on your purchase, and if this will be your primary residence, return the Assessor’s claim as soon as it arrives. If you’re buying a second home or an investment property, plan for the up-to-8% cap instead.
