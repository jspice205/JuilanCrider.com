The Nevada “Duties Owed” form: what you’re signing and why it protects you

Whether you’re buying or selling in Nevada, one of the first documents your agent hands you is a short form from the Nevada Real Estate Division: “Duties Owed by a Nevada Real Estate Licensee,” often called Form 525. It isn’t a contract to hire anyone. It’s a disclosure that tells you what every agent in the transaction owes you under Nevada law.

1. Why you’re seeing it

Nevada law (NRS 645.252) requires an agent to give this form to each party they represent, and to anyone in the transaction who doesn’t have their own agent. Signing it confirms you received it. It doesn’t lock you into working with that agent.

2. Duties every agent owes everyone

An agent can’t deal with any party in a way that is deceitful, fraudulent or dishonest, and must use reasonable skill and care with all parties, not just their own client. That includes the other side of the deal.

3. Material facts get disclosed

Agents must disclose material facts about the property that they know, or should reasonably know, as soon as practicable. If something important about the home comes to light, you should hear about it.

4. Extra duties to the agent’s own client

The form also lists what an agent owes the person they represent, such as keeping confidential information confidential and accounting for any money they handle. These are the duties that make it worth having your own agent.

5. One agent for both sides needs your written consent

If one agent wants to represent both the buyer and the seller, Nevada requires written consent from each party. That consent has to describe the transaction and say plainly that the two sides’ interests are adverse and the agent has a conflict.

6. What an agent doesn’t have to do

Unless you agree otherwise in writing, an agent isn’t required to independently verify what an inspector says, investigate a party’s finances, or inspect the property’s condition themselves. That’s why your own inspection and your own lender matter.
